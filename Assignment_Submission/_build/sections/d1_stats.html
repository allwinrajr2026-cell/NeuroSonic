Problem Substantiation with Statistics

Global burden

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that, globally, at least 2.2 billion people have a near or distance vision impairment, and that for at least 1 billion of them the impairment could have been prevented or has yet to be addressed [[who]]. The 2.2-billion figure was first published in WHO's World Report on Vision in October 2019 [[whoreport]]. Among people with distance vision impairment or blindness, the leading causes are cataract (94 million), refractive error (88.4 million), age-related macular degeneration (8 million), glaucoma (7.7 million) and diabetic retinopathy (3.9 million); near vision impairment is mainly due to presbyopia (826 million) [[who]]. {{f-who}} shows the distance-vision causes.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Vision Loss Expert Group estimated that in 2020, 43.3 million people were blind (95 % uncertainty interval 37.6–48.4 million), of whom 23.9 million (55 %) were female, and that 295 million people had moderate or severe vision impairment [[gbd]]. Between 1990 and 2020 the age-standardised prevalence of blindness among adults aged 50 years and older fell by 28.5 %, yet the number of blind people rose by 50.6 %, because populations grew and aged. The same study projects 61.0 million blind people and 474 million with moderate or severe vision impairment by 2050 [[gbd]] ({{f-gbd}}). In other words, medical progress is reducing the risk for each person, but the absolute number of people who need mobility support is still increasing.

The situation in India

India's National Blindness and Visual Impairment Survey 2015–2019, run under the National Programme for Control of Blindness and Visual Impairment with AIIMS, surveyed people aged 50 years and older in 31 districts [[nbvis]]. It reports a national prevalence of blindness of 1.99 % in this age group, with cataract the major cause (66.2 % of blindness), and a 47.1 % reduction in blindness compared with the 2010 baseline [[nbvis,nbvis2]]. Prevalence rises steeply with age: 0.5 % at 50–59 years, 1.6 % at 60–69, 4.1 % at 70–79 and 11.6 % at 80 years and above [[nbvis2]] ({{f-india}}). District values vary considerably, which matters for any local deployment [[nbvis]].

Three implications follow. First, the user population is large, growing and old. A device for this population must be affordable and must be operable by people who may not be familiar with technology; speech is the most natural channel. Second, most of these users live in low- and middle-income countries, where the cost of dedicated hardware (several thousand US dollars for some commercial wearables, see the product survey) is a serious barrier, whereas a smartphone is often already owned. Third, because vision impairment is often partial and acquired late in life, people continue to rely on the cane. An electronic aid should supplement, not replace, the cane, and must never claim more than it can deliver. This last point is central to the design principles of NeuroSonic: it never states distances, never says that a way is "safe", and never claims 360-degree awareness.

The statistics do not, by themselves, measure the frequency of collisions or falls caused by obstacles while walking; we did not find a recent, population-level Indian statistic on that question that we could verify, so we do not quote one. The base paper's comparison with the white cane [[woad]] is an experimental result from twelve participants, not a population statistic, and is quoted only as an illustration.
