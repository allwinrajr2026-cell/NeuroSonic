Survey: Comparative Analysis of Existing Solutions (Publications)

Method of the survey

Publications were selected to cover the main families of approaches: (i) review papers that map the field; (ii) wearable RGB-D or ToF systems with on-body or phone processing; (iii) robot guides; (iv) segmentation-based "walkable area" methods; and (v) the base paper. Each was checked against the publisher, PubMed Central or arXiv record. Where only the abstract was accessible, we report only what the abstract states and do not infer numbers. The comparison criteria are: sensors, where the computation runs, perception method, user feedback, navigation support, evaluation, and the main limitation relevant to a low-cost, phone-based system.

Review papers

Real and Araujo [[real2019]] review navigation systems for the blind from the first sonar-based ETAs and sensory substitution devices to smartphone, wearable and cloud-based designs. They identify recurring challenges: a heterogeneous user population, the limited bandwidth of hearing and touch, high cognitive load, poor acceptance of past devices, and a trade-off between positioning accuracy and infrastructure cost. Kuriakose, Shrestha and Sandnes [[kuriakose2020]] survey tools and technologies for navigation support and give design recommendations for future systems. Both reviews stress that technical performance alone does not guarantee adoption, which is why user-facing behaviour (what is said, when, and how reliably) is treated as a first-class requirement in this project.

Wearable depth-camera systems

WOAD (base paper) [[woad]] is the most complete recent system. Self-developed glasses (about 400 g including an 80 g battery) carry a Synexens CS30 sensor with a near-infrared camera and a ToF depth sensor (640×480, 0.1–5 m). A depth-aided compression module, whose policy is learned with a discrete soft actor-critic reinforcement-learning agent [[dsac]], decides which frames to send and at what quality; it runs on a custom FPGA board. A smartphone (Samsung Galaxy S23 Ultra) runs a YOLOv5-based [[yolov5]] detector extended with a second input stream and transformer blocks [[transformer]] that fuse infrared and depth features ("cross-modal learning"). Alerts are spoken (attention/warning/danger) and given by three vibration motors that encode direction and distance. The authors report 100 % collision avoidance in their indoor and outdoor scenarios, an end-to-end delay below 320 ms, about 11 h of operation, and a System Usability Scale score of 83.75 with twelve participants providing quantitative data [[woad]]. It needs the custom glasses and the CS30 sensor.

Bai et al. [[bai2019]] built a glasses-shaped travel aid with a consumer RGB-D camera and an inertial measurement unit. Ground segmentation separates the walkable ground from obstacles; a CNN on a smartphone classifies objects. The walkable direction is conveyed by beeps and object categories by speech. Twenty visually impaired participants navigated an office environment and avoided collisions; the abstract gives no quantitative accuracy or latency [[bai2019]]. Lin et al. [[lin2019]] combine an RGB-D wearable terminal, an earphone and a smartphone touch interface with a separate, powerful processor; a learned model predicts walkable instructions from RGB-D data and a semantic map. They report good results on indoor and outdoor datasets with low-lying obstacles and a user study, without numbers in the abstract.

Xu, Song and Wang [[xu2023]] present a head-mounted obstacle-avoidance wearable (an extended version of a 2023 conference paper). Tang et al. [[tang2025]] describe a human-centred wearable assistance system: an RGB-D camera on a 3D-printed glasses frame, ultrathin "artificial skins" with haptic actuators on the wrist and fingers, a bone-conduction earphone and smart insoles, with all computation local. It was tested with humanoid robots and with 20 visually impaired participants without prior experience of such systems; the authors argue that human adaptation has been under-considered in earlier systems [[tang2025]]. Yan et al. [[yan2025]] propose gait-based guidance using multimodal sensing and report better performance than a guide cane in indoor and outdoor experiments (preprint; no numbers in the abstract). AviPer [[aviper]] uses a visual-tactile multimodal attention network.

Robot guides and segmentation approaches

CaBot [[cabot]] is a suitcase-shaped autonomous navigation robot that guides blind users to destinations in unfamiliar indoor environments while avoiding obstacles, with a vibro-tactile handle; it was evaluated with ten blind users. It moves the sensing burden from the person to a robot, at the cost of carrying a robot. Ghahremanians and Mahvash Mohammadi [[ghahr2025]] take a segmentation view: instead of detecting individual obstacles, a panoptic network finds the safe walking area and treats everything else as an obstacle. Their model reaches a panoptic quality of 37.6 on Cityscapes at about 4.3 frames/s (232 ms) on an RTX 4090, trained on a custom dataset that is not public.

Observations

  • Depth is the norm in research systems. Nearly all wearable systems above use RGB-D or ToF sensors, which measure distance directly. A phone RGB camera does not, so a phone-based system must either avoid distance claims or estimate depth by other means.
  • Custom hardware limits reach. Glasses, FPGAs, artificial skins and robots deliver strong results in studies but are not available to most users, especially in India.
  • Obstacle detection and navigation are rarely combined. Most systems focus on the next few metres; route guidance is handled separately (e.g. by phone map apps), if at all.
  • Motion semantics are under-explored. The surveyed detectors work frame by frame. Telling the user that a person is moving into the path, while not confusing this with the user's own walking, requires tracking and camera-motion compensation [[sort,bytetrack]].
  • Reproducibility is uneven. Several works report results on private datasets; we found in re-implementing the base paper that its published dataset differs from the one behind its reported accuracy (Review 2).
