Abstract

Pedestrians with visual impairment need to know both what is in front of them now and where to turn. This report consolidates three reviews of one project. Review 1 substantiates the problem with WHO, Global Burden of Disease and Indian survey statistics (at least 2.2 billion people with vision impairment; 43.3 million blind in 2020 and 61 million projected by 2050; 1.99 % blindness among Indians aged 50 and over) and compares published systems, nine verified patents and eight commercial products, followed by five technology trends. Review 2 re-implements the base paper, WOAD (Gao et al., Nature Communications, 2025), a near-infrared + depth cross-modal detector. The standalone re-implementation is bit-identical to the original, but the reported accuracy cannot be reproduced with the published data, the cross-dataset person AP@0.5 is 0.218, detections collapse on darker sensor recordings, and the model cannot be driven by a phone camera. Eight gaps are derived. Review 3 presents NeuroSonic, in which an iPhone browser is the camera, microphone, GPS receiver and speaker of a system that runs offline on a laptop GPU. RF-DETR Nano, selected among four detectors for its person AP@0.5 (0.660) and recall (0.729), feeds a camera-motion-compensated tracker that announces people or vehicles entering or crossing the walking path without reacting to the user's own movement. Offline neural speech, voice commands and OpenStreetMap routing complete the system. On the laptop it sustained about 5 frames/s with a median capture-to-answer time of 67 ms; three serving problems were found and fixed; ten-minute soak tests ran without errors; 204 automated tests and 7 browser tests pass. Field validation with an iPhone and users is pending; the protocol for it is included. The system is a research prototype and not a safety device.

Keywords: assistive technology; visual impairment; obstacle detection; RF-DETR; multi-object tracking; camera-motion compensation; offline speech; pedestrian navigation; reproducibility.
