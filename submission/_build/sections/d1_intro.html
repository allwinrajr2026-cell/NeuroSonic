Introduction: Domain and Field of Work

This work belongs to assistive technology for people with visual impairment, and within it to the sub-field of electronic travel aids (ETAs) and navigation aids. An ETA is a device that senses the environment ahead of a pedestrian and turns that information into a signal the person can hear or feel. A navigation aid tells the person where they are and which way to turn to reach a destination. The two needs are related but different: the first is about the next few metres and the next few seconds, the second about the whole journey [[real2019,kuriakose2020]].

For decades the white cane and the guide dog have been the primary mobility aids. The cane is cheap, reliable, and gives immediate physical feedback about the ground, but its range is roughly the length of the cane, it does not detect obstacles at head or chest height, and it does not tell the user what an object is or whether it is moving. Guide dogs address some of these limits but are scarce and costly to train. The base paper of this project reports that, in its own trials, users with a white cane avoided only about a quarter of suddenly appearing indoor obstacles, and about 40 % of fast-moving cars at night [[woad]]. These figures come from a specific experimental set-up, but they illustrate why supplementary sensing is studied at all.

The field draws on several engineering disciplines at once:

  • Sensing: ultrasonic range finders, infrared and time-of-flight (ToF) depth cameras, ordinary RGB cameras, inertial sensors and satellite positioning (GNSS/GPS) [[real2019]].
  • Perception: object detection, semantic and panoptic segmentation, tracking and scene description, today dominated by deep neural networks [[yolo,detr,ghahr2025]].
  • Human–computer interaction: how to present information through hearing (speech, spatial audio, tones) or touch (vibration, braille) without overloading a sense that the user already relies on to move safely [[real2019,tang2025]].
  • Embedded and edge computing: running perception within the power, weight, latency and cost limits of a wearable or a phone, sometimes with dedicated hardware such as FPGAs [[woad]].
  • Navigation: maps, routing and localisation, both outdoors (GNSS) and indoors (beacons, visual odometry) [[pat_aph,pat_brown]].

Over the last decade, three changes have reshaped the field. First, deep-learning detectors made it possible to recognise many object classes in real time on modest hardware [[yolo,ssd,mbv3]]. Second, smartphones became capable sensing and computing platforms that most users already carry, which lowers the cost and the social stigma of a dedicated device [[kuriakose2020]]. Third, large vision-language models have made free-form scene description available as a cloud service, which is now offered in consumer apps for blind users [[p_perkins]].

The present project is called NeuroSonic. It started from the base paper WOAD: a wearable obstacle avoidance device for visually impaired individuals with cross-modal learning, published in Nature Communications in 2025 [[woad]]. WOAD combines self-developed glasses with a near-infrared camera and a ToF depth sensor, a reinforcement-learning video compression module, an FPGA, and a smartphone that runs a cross-modal (infrared + depth) obstacle detector. NeuroSonic re-implements and evaluates the WOAD detector, identifies the gaps that prevent it from being used with ordinary phones, and proposes and implements a phone-camera system with spoken guidance that runs offline on a laptop. This first review covers the domain, the size of the problem, and a comparative survey of publications, patents and products, followed by the technology trends that shape the proposed work.

The scope of this survey is deliberately bounded. It covers systems that sense the environment in front of a walking person and communicate with the person in real time, plus the navigation aids they are combined with. It does not cover reading aids (text recognition for documents) except where they are part of the same product, and it does not cover medical treatment of vision loss. The patents and products listed are those that could be verified from primary pages (patent office records, publisher pages, manufacturer or established-retailer pages); they are a sample of the field, not an exhaustive census.
