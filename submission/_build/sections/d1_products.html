Survey: Comparative Analysis of Company Products

Products were chosen to represent the four commercial categories: smart canes, wearable cameras, smartphone apps and robotic or harness guides. Prices are quoted only where an established retailer or a consumer organisation published them, and they change frequently; they are indicative.

Smart canes

WeWALK is a smart handle that attaches to a standard white cane. It detects obstacles above chest level with an ultrasonic sensor and warns with vibration, connects to a smartphone, and has a touch pad for features such as public-transport information; battery life is quoted as up to five hours, and AARP lists it at US$599 [[p_aarp,p_wewalk]]. SmartCane, developed at IIT Delhi's Assistech laboratory with the Saksham Trust and manufactured by Phoenix Medical Systems, attaches to a white cane and uses ultrasonic sensing to detect obstacles above knee height, signalling distance with vibration patterns; it has long-range (about 3 m) and short-range (about 1.8 m) modes, and its translational development was funded by the Wellcome Trust [[p_smartcane]]. Smart canes extend the cane's range upwards, but they measure distance only; they do not recognise objects or describe motion.

Wearable cameras

OrCam MyEye is a small camera that clips magnetically onto eyeglasses and works offline, without a smartphone or Wi-Fi; it reads text and, in the Pro version, recognises faces, colours and bank notes, controlled by gestures and voice commands. AARP lists the MyEye Pro at US$4,250; RNIB lists the Pro at £3,700 and the cheaper Smart version at £2,700 (both ex VAT) [[p_aarp,p_rnib]]. It is a reading and recognition aid rather than a mobility aid.

Smartphone apps

Microsoft Seeing AI is free, with no subscriptions or in-app purchases, and organises tasks into "channels" (reading text, identifying products, recognising currency) [[p_perkins]]. Google Lookout (Android) offers several modes, including an Explore mode that identifies objects on the device, plus text and product recognition [[p_lookout]]. Be My Eyes connects a blind user by live video to a sighted volunteer, and its Be My AI feature describes a photo and lets the user ask questions about it [[p_perkins]]. These apps are free or low-cost and use a phone the user already has, but they work on single photos or on demand, need the user to point the phone, and several of their richer functions need the internet.

Harness and robotic guides

biped.ai NOA is a shoulder-worn harness with depth cameras covering a wide field of view (a 170° field is reported) that predicts object trajectories a few seconds ahead and warns with 3-D sound; a RealSense case study states that it uses the D430 depth module [[p_noa]]. Glidance Glide is a two-wheeled, self-guided mobility aid pushed by the user, which uses cameras and sensors to steer around hazards; prices reported in early 2025 were US$1,500 plus a monthly subscription, and it was still in development and testing at the time of the reports [[p_glide]].

Observations from the product survey

  • A price gap. Dedicated wearables cost from about US$600 (cane handle) to over US$4,000 (wearable camera), whereas the phone apps are free. A system that uses hardware people already own addresses the affordability issue raised by the statistics.
  • A capability gap in the free apps. The free apps recognise and describe, but on demand; they do not continuously warn about objects moving into the walking path. Continuous, motion-aware warning is offered only by dedicated hardware such as NOA.
  • Connectivity. Richer descriptions depend on the internet; mobility support should not, because connectivity is unreliable exactly where people walk.
