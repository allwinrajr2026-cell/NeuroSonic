Serving details mattered more than the detector's benchmark speed. A detector that takes 21 ms back to back took several times longer at 5 frames/s, because of idle CPU threads, per-request threads and, above all, GPU clock scaling at low load. Benchmarks for assistive systems should therefore be run at the application's frame rate, not only back to back.

Motion without false alarms has a price. In tests with a synthetically panning camera, a static car gave no event while a person moving into the path did. But events need about one second of confirmation and are suppressed while the camera sways strongly, which can include normal walking with a hand-held phone. Missed events are therefore expected; this is acceptable only because the system supplements the cane.

Comparison with the base paper. WOAD reports field results with users and a custom device [[woad]]; NeuroSonic reports laptop and browser results with a phone-based design that any user with a phone and a laptop could run. The two are complementary: WOAD shows what dedicated depth hardware can achieve in the field, while NeuroSonic removes the hardware barrier and adds motion events, navigation and speech input, but has yet to be validated in the field.

Limitations and Pending Validation

  • No formal iPhone or field validation yet. RF-DETR has processed real iPhone frames only in informal screenshot checks; Wi-Fi latency, outdoor light, and use by visually impaired people are not yet measured systematically.
  • Plain surfaces. In an informal iPhone check a textureless wall produced “Stop” although no object class was detected; the blocked-view rule treats an uninformative view as blocked. Its false-alarm rate on real surfaces is unmeasured.
  • Proxy data. Detector quality was measured on near-infrared images with 129 people; live tests use a fake camera and a composited clip.
  • Scope. 23 COCO classes only: no steps, kerbs, holes, poles, walls or wet floors; no distance and no "approaching" alerts.
  • Dependence. The phone needs the laptop and local Wi-Fi; iOS Safari stops the camera when the screen locks; the GPU path needs an NVIDIA GPU.
  • Single machine. All timings come from one laptop and driver.
  • Cloud description. The single approved live request timed out; cloud quality and latency are unmeasured.

Conclusion

NeuroSonic shows that a consumer laptop GPU can give a browser-based phone client real-time, offline, spoken awareness of objects and of movement into the walking path, together with offline navigation and voice commands. In our tests it sustained about 5 frames/s and answered within roughly 50–100 ms on the laptop. The general lessons are to measure at the application's frame rate, to constrain CPU threads when the GPU does the work, and to keep GPU work on one thread. The next step is not a new model but evidence: the iPhone and field protocol, followed by monocular depth for "approaching" alerts, ground-hazard data under open licences, and a native application with on-device inference that removes the laptop dependency. NeuroSonic is a research prototype and not a safety device.
